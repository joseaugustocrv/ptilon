# ISO/IEC 25010 — Product Quality

## 1. Purpose

Document how ISO/IEC 25010 is used as the primary product quality reference
within Ptilon.

This document provides a Ptilon interpretation and does not reproduce the
standard.

---

## 2. Reference

**ISO/IEC 25010 — Systems and software engineering — Systems and software
Quality Requirements and Evaluation (SQuaRE) — Product quality model**

Official reference: <https://www.iso.org/standard/78176.html>

The applicable edition should be explicitly identified when a project has
contractual, regulatory, or formal conformance requirements.

---

## 3. Role in Ptilon

ISO/IEC 25010 provides the primary quality model for Ptilon.

Ptilon uses the model to ensure that software quality is considered beyond
functional behavior and that relevant quality characteristics are translated
into explicit, verifiable requirements.

Quality is treated as a property of the product and its behavior in its
intended context of use.

---

## 4. Quality characteristics

The current ISO/IEC 25010 product quality model identifies the following
characteristics:

- functional suitability;
- performance efficiency;
- compatibility;
- interaction capability;
- reliability;
- security;
- maintainability;
- flexibility;
- safety.

Projects should determine which characteristics are relevant to their context
rather than assuming that all characteristics require the same level of
attention.

---

## 5. Quality requirements

Quality characteristics should be translated into measurable or otherwise
verifiable quality requirements when they are relevant.

Examples include:

- response time targets;
- throughput requirements;
- availability objectives;
- recovery requirements;
- access control requirements;
- maintainability constraints;
- compatibility requirements;
- usability or interaction requirements;
- portability or adaptability expectations;
- safety constraints.

Statements such as “the system must be fast” are insufficient when a
measurable criterion is necessary.

---

## 6. Quality attributes and acceptance criteria

Relevant quality requirements should be reflected in acceptance criteria.

A typical chain is:

**Quality Concern → Quality Requirement → Acceptance Criterion →
Verification Method → Evidence**

Verification methods may include:

- automated tests;
- performance tests;
- security tests;
- static analysis;
- inspection;
- compatibility tests;
- operational measurements;
- controlled evaluation.

---

## 7. AI-assisted quality engineering

AI may assist with:

- identifying relevant quality characteristics;
- drafting quality requirements;
- generating test cases;
- identifying edge cases;
- analyzing test coverage;
- reviewing specifications;
- detecting inconsistencies;
- analyzing evidence.

AI-generated quality artifacts require appropriate human validation.

AI should not be assumed to identify all relevant quality risks or to provide
reliable measurements without verification.

---

## 8. Quality by risk

Quality engineering should be proportional to the consequences of failure.

Factors that may increase the required level of rigor include:

- business criticality;
- security exposure;
- regulatory obligations;
- financial impact;
- operational impact;
- number of users;
- system complexity;
- integration complexity;
- architectural novelty;
- change scope.

Higher-risk characteristics should receive stronger requirements, verification,
and evidence.

---

## 9. Quality throughout the lifecycle

Quality is not a final inspection activity.

Ptilon integrates quality throughout its canonical lifecycle:

**Intent → Requirements → Specification → Architecture & Design → Plan →
Tasks → Implementation → Verification → Convergence → Release**

Examples:

- **Requirements:** identify quality requirements;
- **Specification:** make quality expectations testable;
- **Architecture & Design:** address relevant quality attributes;
- **Plan:** define verification strategy;
- **Implementation:** apply engineering controls;
- **Verification:** generate quality evidence;
- **Convergence:** resolve remaining quality gaps;
- **Release:** confirm applicable quality criteria.

Reviews are cross-cutting quality activities rather than an additional
lifecycle stage.

---

## 10. Quality gates

Before release, the project should establish that applicable quality
requirements have been:

- identified;
- translated into verifiable criteria;
- addressed by implementation;
- verified using appropriate methods;
- supported by evidence;
- accepted or explicitly waived when necessary.

A quality gate should not be reduced to a simple count of passed tests.

---

## 11. Functional correctness versus product quality

Functional correctness is necessary but does not by itself establish overall
product quality.

A system may satisfy functional requirements while still exhibiting
unacceptable:

- performance;
- reliability;
- security;
- maintainability;
- compatibility;
- interaction quality;
- safety characteristics.

Ptilon therefore treats functional and quality requirements as complementary
dimensions.

---

## 12. Quality and architecture

Quality requirements can have significant architectural consequences.

For example:

- performance requirements may influence architecture and data access;
- availability requirements may influence redundancy and recovery;
- security requirements may influence identity and trust boundaries;
- maintainability requirements may influence modularity;
- compatibility requirements may influence interfaces and integration design.

Relevant quality-driven architectural decisions should be recorded as ADRs.

---

## 13. Quality and change impact

When a requirement or implementation changes, affected quality characteristics
should be reconsidered.

Impact analysis should consider whether the change affects:

- existing quality requirements;
- quality acceptance criteria;
- architecture;
- test strategy;
- operational behavior;
- previously collected evidence.

A change should not be considered isolated merely because its visible
functional scope is small.

---

## 14. Conformance

Using ISO/IEC 25010 as a reference does not by itself establish conformance.

A formal conformance claim requires identification of the applicable edition,
applicable requirements, implementation evidence, and an appropriate
assessment process.

Project documentation must distinguish between:

- reference use;
- adopted quality practices;
- contractual requirements;
- formal conformance.

---

## 15. Ptilon implications

The ISO/IEC 25010 reference establishes several principles for Ptilon:

1. quality must be considered throughout the lifecycle;
2. relevant quality characteristics should be explicit;
3. quality requirements should be verifiable;
4. quality should influence architecture and implementation decisions;
5. verification should generate evidence;
6. quality rigor should be proportional to risk;
7. AI-generated quality artifacts require validation.
